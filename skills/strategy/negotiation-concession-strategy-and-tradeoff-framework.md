# Skill Name: Negotiation Concession Strategy and Tradeoff Framework

## 🎯 Objective

Requires every negotiation — a customer or partner proposal, a vendor contract, a product-terms discussion, or any similar give-and-take with a counterpart — to open with a **pre-agreed three-tier concession strategy**, so no concession is ever improvised under live pressure. Every negotiable topic is classified before the session into exactly one tier:

1. **Free Trade-Aways** — topics the team can give up at no financial cost and no risk to the firm or the deal. They exist to give the counterpart a genuine sense of having won something in return. Agreed in advance; never a financial concession.
2. **Priced Trades** — topics the team is willing to negotiate because they are genuinely valuable, but never for free. Any movement needs a good reason and a stated exchange, because it carries financial or other impact.
3. **Protected Terms** — topics the team does not trade, or trades only after a high degree of justification and senior approval, because they raise risk significantly or would definitively become a financial burden.

This is the workspace's **preparation and concession-discipline** skill for negotiation. `ooda-loop-decision-cycle.md` names negotiation as a use case but governs decision *tempo* against an adapting counterpart; it does not say what to concede, in what order, or what must never move. This skill does. Run this one first; run OODA alongside it when the counterpart is fast-moving or adversarial.

**Standing rule: strategy before the room.** A negotiation does not open until the three-tier sheet exists and the people with authority over each tier have agreed to it. "We'll see how it goes" is not a strategy; it is how Tier 3 terms get conceded in the last ten minutes of a long session.

## 👤 Target Persona

Product Manager, Product Owner, and any representative team (Deal Desk, Sales Leadership, Partnerships, Procurement, Account Team) preparing to negotiate a proposal, contract, or product topic — and the executive or finance partner who must pre-approve the sheet.

## 📥 Inputs Required

- **The counterpart and the deal**: who is on the other side, what is being negotiated (customer proposal, vendor/partner contract, product scope/terms), and what a "good" outcome is for us.
- **The full issue list**: every topic likely to come up — price, discounts, payment terms, contract length, renewal, SLAs, liability/indemnity, IP, data/security/compliance obligations, scope, timeline, support level, roadmap commitments, exclusivity, and anything else the counterpart has signaled.
- **Our cost and risk per topic**: financial impact of conceding it (use `../financial-impact-analysis/cost-based-pricing-floor-and-margin-governance.md` for anything price-related), delivery/capacity impact, and legal/security/compliance exposure.
- **What the counterpart values**: their stated positions *and* the interests behind them, as far as known or inferable. Topics they care about that cost us little are the raw material for Tier 1.
- **Our alternative if the deal fails (BATNA)** and, where estimable, theirs.
- **Approval authority**: who signs off on the strategy sheet, who can approve a Tier 2 movement, and who can approve a Tier 3 exception.

**Minimum viable input:** what you want, what you believe they want, and your walk-away. Without a BATNA the tier sheet can still be built, but flag the missing BATNA as the single highest-priority gap — every concession decision in the session depends on it, and it is the one input that cannot be improvised once the session starts.

## 📤 Expected Output

- A completed **Strategy Sheet** (template below): every topic assigned to exactly one tier, each Tier 1 item shown to have passed four tests, each Tier 2 item carrying a movement range and required exchange, each Tier 3 item marked hard wall or gated-exception.
- A stated BATNA / walk-away position and a ZOPA check.
- Evidence the sheet was **agreed in advance** by the named authorities — not just drafted.
- During and after the session: a **Concession Ledger** recording every concession, the tier it came from, and what was received in return.
- A post-session reconciliation: every Tier 3 exception has its approval and risk owner recorded, and the final deal is checked against the cost floor.

## The Three Tiers in Detail

### Tier 1 — Free Trade-Aways

**Purpose:** these are *currency*, not concessions of substance. They let the counterpart leave with something they can point to as a win. The mechanism is the norm of reciprocity: when one side is seen to give something, the other feels pressure to respond in kind, and each side expects concessions to be returned. Concessions only trigger that response if they are visible and perceived as valued.

An item qualifies for Tier 1 only if it passes **all four** tests (workspace design rules):

| Test | Question | Fails if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Zero-Cost** | Is the total financial impact nil, including support load, delivery effort, and recurring or downstream cost? | Any real cost exists, even small and recurring. It belongs in Tier 2. |
| **Zero-Risk** | Does it create no legal, security, compliance, delivery, or precedent risk to the firm or the deal? | It sets a precedent that later erodes a Tier 2 or 3 position. |
| **Pre-Agreed** | Was it approved *before* the session by a named authority? | Anyone would need to check with someone mid-session. |
| **Perceived-Value** | Does the counterpart actually value it, as best we can tell? | They would shrug. A give they don't value buys nothing. |

**How to use it:**

- **Hold it back as currency.** Releasing all Tier 1 items up front spends the reciprocity effect before there is anything to reciprocate against.
- **Tie each give to a request.** Offer it in the same breath as an ask ("we can do X — can we settle Y?"), not as an unprompted gift. The door-in-the-face research found the reciprocal effect weakened with a delay between the concession and the follow-up ask; applying that here is an inference from that research, not a tested finding about Tier 1 itself.
- **Do not invent Tier 1 items to fill a quota.** If the counterpart values none of what we can give free, the answer is to learn their interests better, not to fabricate currency.

Illustrative candidates only — each must pass the four tests in *your* context, since something free for one team carries support cost for another: invoice format or consolidation, named-contact arrangements, review or check-in cadence, ordering of milestones, non-binding announcement timing, contract-document formatting and notice mechanics.

### Tier 2 — Priced Trades

**Purpose:** topics that matter to us and are worth negotiating, but that cost something real. The team has decided in advance that movement is acceptable *within a range and for a reason*.

For every Tier 2 topic, the sheet holds a **topic card**:

- **Why it matters** to us (value at stake).
- **Opening position, target, and limit** — the pre-agreed movement range. The limit is the furthest we will go *within this tier*; beyond it the topic is effectively Tier 3.
- **Cost of movement at each step** — financial and non-financial (delivery, risk, precedent).
- **The reason we may move** — what justifies movement: a reciprocal give from the counterpart, a larger or longer commitment, or an objective criterion (market benchmark, precedent) that makes the move principled rather than arbitrary.
- **What we ask for in return** — a short list of acceptable trades, ideally on topics the counterpart values differently from us.
- **Who approves** movement to the limit.

**Rules of movement:**

- **Never free.** A Tier 2 move made without an exchange is a Tier 2 topic being treated as Tier 1. Log it as a breach of the sheet.
- **Trade across topics.** Give ground on what we value less in exchange for ground on what we value more (logrolling). Negotiate several topics together and propose packages, rather than settling them one at a time where nothing is left to trade against.
- **Every move is conditional and logged.** "If you can do Y, we can do X." The Concession Ledger records what was given and what was received.
- **Reason first.** Before moving, be able to state what we get, what it costs, and why the trade is net-positive. If we can't, we don't move.

### Tier 3 — Protected Terms

**Purpose:** topics where conceding would significantly increase risk or definitively become a financial burden. The default answer is no. Each Tier 3 item is marked either:

- **Hard wall — no exception path.** Crossing it means walking away. Reserve for terms where no justification could outweigh the downside.
- **Gated exception — high-justification path only.** Movement is possible, but only through the exception path below, never in the room.

Illustrative Tier 3 candidates (each organization configures its own): pricing below total-cost breakeven; any line item with negative ROI; uncapped liability or indemnity; security, privacy, or compliance obligations the firm cannot meet or would be exposed by; ownership of core IP; delivery or capacity commitments the team cannot actually fulfil; terms that create open-ended cost.

**Classify by consequence, not by how hard the counterpart pushes.** How much they want a term says nothing about how dangerous it is for us to give it.

**The exception path** (workspace design rules — all steps required):

1. **Pause; don't decide live.** Take a break or take the request away. Tier 3 is never resolved under in-room pressure.
2. **Find the interest behind the ask.** Ask what the counterpart actually needs and look for an option that meets the need without the Tier 3 term.
3. **Quantify the worst case** — financial downside and risk exposure if the exception goes wrong. For any price-floor breach use `../financial-impact-analysis/cost-based-pricing-floor-and-margin-governance.md`, whose absolute rules already say a floor breach is a different kind of decision needing explicit escalation, not a larger discount approval.
4. **Write the justification**: the strategic value that exceeds the worst case, the compensating consideration received, and the containment (cap, term limit, carve-out).
5. **Get approval above the room** from the named Tier 3 authority, in writing.
6. **Record the risk and owner** (an approved exception is an Accepted risk with a named owner — see `../communication/roam-risk-communication.md`) and the decision rationale (`../communication/architecture-decision-records.md`).
7. **An exception is not a reclassification.** The next negotiation starts with the item in Tier 3 again unless the playbook is formally revised by the same authority.

**No tier drift in the room.** Nothing is promoted or demoted between tiers mid-session. Reclassification happens outside the session, by the sheet's approving authority, so real-time pressure cannot quietly relabel a Tier 3 item as Tier 2.

## Anchoring The Tiers In Established Negotiation Concepts

The tiers are this workspace's operating structure; they sit on top of established concepts rather than replacing them:

| Concept | Role in this framework |
| --- | --- |
| **BATNA** (Fisher & Ury) — best alternative if no deal is reached | Grounds Tier 3. It is what makes a walk-away credible and sets the minimum acceptable deal. |
| **Reservation point / ZOPA** — worst acceptable terms; the range where both sides' minimums overlap | Run the ZOPA check up front. With no overlap, no rational agreement exists unless the negotiation is *enlarged* with more topics — never bridged by breaching Tier 3. |
| **Reciprocity / reciprocal concessions** | The psychological mechanism behind Tier 1. |
| **Logrolling** — trading across topics valued differently | The mechanism behind Tier 2 exchanges, and the source of Tier 1 items (cheap to us, valuable to them). |
| **Interests, not positions; objective criteria** (Fisher & Ury) | How we discover what they value, and how we justify Tier 2 moves as principled. |

**BATNA caution:** the source notes most managers overestimate their BATNA and invest too little in researching their real options. Before treating a walk-away as credible, ask what actually happens to the firm if we walk.

## Applying The Same Tiers To Internal Tradeoffs

The structure works when the "counterpart" is an internal stakeholder — scope vs. deadline, feature vs. margin, speed vs. quality. Tier 1 is what can be dropped at no cost; Tier 2 is what moves only against a stated exchange; Tier 3 is what the team protects (e.g., a compliance obligation, a quality bar, a cost floor). Two differences: the reciprocity dynamics matter less among collaborators, and transparency matters more. Don't stage-manage an internal Tier 1 give as a sacrifice.

## Honesty And Limits

- **Never misrepresent a free give as a costly one.** Tier 1 works because the counterpart genuinely receives something they value. Saying "this is a big concession for us" about something that costs nothing is deception, not strategy. Frame it truthfully as a trade: "we can do X if you can do Y."
- **This framework does not rely on padded asks.** Door-in-the-face research found the technique works only when the first request seems reasonable rather than deliberately extreme, and extreme openings can backfire. Nothing here requires inflating a first position.
- **Logrolling has limits.** It is ineffective with a single issue, when both sides want the same thing, without preparation on our own priorities, and with dishonest counterparts. Add issues, or treat it as a distributive negotiation governed by BATNA and Tier 3 alone.
- **Prepared tiers are not a script.** Fisher & Ury's framework is criticized for downplaying distributive bargaining, assuming unrealistic rationality, and applying unevenly across cultures and group dynamics. If new information changes what the counterpart values or what a topic costs us, stop and update the sheet through the approving authority; do not improvise in the room.
- **The tests and rules above are workspace design**, built to make the three-tier structure enforceable. They are not findings from the sources cited below.

## 🤖 Core Prompt / Instructions

```text
You are a negotiation-strategy advisor for a product or representative team. Your
job is to produce a pre-agreed three-tier concession strategy BEFORE a negotiation
opens, and to enforce it during and after. Never help the team open a negotiation
without one.

0. CONFIRM SCOPE.
   Confirm there is a real counterpart and a real give-and-take (customer/partner
   proposal, vendor contract, product terms, or an internal scope/date tradeoff).
   If a formal decision between AI agents is involved, route to
   `orchestration/conflict-and-consensus-resolution.md` instead.

1. INVENTORY EVERY TOPIC.
   List all issues likely to be raised, including ones the counterpart has hinted
   at. Unlisted topics get conceded by accident.

2. MODEL OUR SIDE.
   For each topic: what it is worth to us, what conceding it costs (financially
   and otherwise), and what risk it carries. Price-related topics: apply the cost
   floor and discount-ladder rules from
   `financial-impact-analysis/cost-based-pricing-floor-and-margin-governance.md`.

3. MODEL THEIR SIDE.
   For each topic: their stated position, the interest behind it, and how much
   they value it. Mark where their valuation differs from ours — those gaps are
   where trades come from. Mark what is unknown; do not guess silently.

4. ASSIGN EXACTLY ONE TIER PER TOPIC.
   - Tier 1 only if it passes ALL FOUR tests: Zero-Cost, Zero-Risk, Pre-Agreed,
     Perceived-Value. If any fails, it is not Tier 1.
   - Tier 2 gets a topic card: value, opening/target/limit, cost per step, reason
     to move, required exchange, approver.
   - Tier 3 is marked HARD WALL or GATED EXCEPTION. Classify by consequence
     (risk, definitive financial burden), never by how hard they push.

5. SET WALK-AWAY AND CHECK THE ZONE.
   State our BATNA concretely and pressure-test it (what really happens if we
   walk?). Estimate theirs. Check whether a ZOPA exists. If not, decide now
   whether to enlarge the negotiation with more topics or walk — do not plan to
   bridge a gap by breaching Tier 3.

6. GET THE SHEET AGREED IN ADVANCE.
   Name who approves Tier 1 (pre-agreed), Tier 2 ranges, and Tier 3 exceptions.
   The sheet is not final until they have signed off. Flag any topic with no
   approver.

7. RUN THE SESSION RULES.
   - Tier 1: hold back as currency; tie each give to an ask; never invent items.
   - Tier 2: never free; every move conditional ("if you X, we Y"); prefer
     multi-topic packages; log every concession in the Concession Ledger.
   - Tier 3: pause, don't decide live; find the interest; quantify the worst
     case; only the exception path may move it. No tier drift in the room.
   - Never misrepresent a free give as costly.

8. RECONCILE AFTERWARD.
   Compare the final deal to the sheet. Every Tier 3 exception must have written
   approval and a named risk owner. Re-run the cost-floor and per-line ROI checks.
   Record what the counterpart valued that we misjudged, and update the playbook
   through the approving authority.

Now apply this to the negotiation:
Counterpart and deal: $DEAL
Issue list: $ISSUES
Our cost/risk per topic: $OUR_COSTS
What the counterpart values: $THEIR_INTERESTS
Our BATNA (and theirs, if known): $BATNA
Approval authorities: $AUTHORITIES
```

## 🔌 Connector Awareness

- **Standalone (always works):** the user pastes or types the deal facts, issue list, and cost/risk notes; the skill produces the complete Strategy Sheet, walk-away check, and session rules from that alone.
- **Supercharged (if connected):** a CRM or deal desk system can supply deal history, prior concessions, and the counterpart's past behavior; a contract repository or e-signature tool can surface prior clauses and past Tier 3 exceptions to seed the protected list; a calendar can lock the pre-session approval review before the negotiation date; a chat tool can circulate the sheet to the named approvers. None of these is required.

## ✅ Success Criteria / Quality Checklist

- [ ] A three-tier sheet existed and was agreed by the named authorities **before** the negotiation opened.
- [ ] Every topic on the issue list has exactly one tier; no topic is unclassified.
- [ ] Every Tier 1 item passes all four tests (Zero-Cost, Zero-Risk, Pre-Agreed, Perceived-Value); none was invented to fill a quota.
- [ ] Every Tier 2 topic has a movement range, cost per step, required exchange, and named approver — and no Tier 2 movement occurred without an exchange.
- [ ] Every Tier 3 item is marked hard wall or gated exception, and was classified by consequence, not counterpart pressure.
- [ ] BATNA is concrete and pressure-tested; a ZOPA check was run, with an enlarge-or-walk decision if none exists.
- [ ] Every concession is in the ledger with what was received in return.
- [ ] No topic changed tier mid-session; any Tier 3 exception has written approval, a justification, containment, and a named risk owner.
- [ ] No free give was presented as a costly sacrifice.
- [ ] The final deal was reconciled against the sheet and the cost floor.
